A video shoot raises a set, actors, and a shift, so the estimate starts in six figures and the first master arrives weeks later. Generation builds the video from a brief: script, frames, voice, and captions, and in our project a product launch ran from script to master in seven days. The choice between video generation and a studio sets the story type and the output volume: short product videos, ads, and training fit a pipeline, while drama with a recurring character stays with a shoot. Below are five criteria, the matrix across them, and a verdict for your task.

## Five criteria and their scale

The two routes compare well on five axes, and each has a scale from one to five. A score rests on a measurement or a named mechanism, so the table below reads as a decision for the task.

1. **Speed to the first master**: calendar days from a signed brief to the finished video. Five is delivery within a week, one is more than a month.
2. **Cost per minute of video**: the estimate for a minute of finished material. A shoot carries the set, the shift, the light, and the actors; generation carries scene payment, take selection, and editing.
3. **Repeatability across a series**: how many videos ship in one style without a new setup. On a shoot the campaign sets the look; on a pipeline a prompt library and references hold it.
4. **The story limit**: the scene type the route holds well, a short format, a product, an explainer, or drama with acting and improvisation.
5. **Legal terms**: what is fixed before the start. Generation fixes model licenses and consent for synthetic footage; a shoot fixes actor and location rights.

Marking is added to the fifth axis. In the EU, disclosure of synthetic content under Article 50 of the AI Act applies from 2 August 2026, so a release for a European audience goes into the publishing rule at the script stage.

## The matrix: generation against a shoot

The scores below use the scale from the previous section, and each rests on a number, a term, or a named limit.

| Axis | Video generation | Studio shoot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed to master | 5: seven days for a launch promo in our project | 2: a shift, editing, and post-production run for weeks |
| Cost per minute | 4: the studio and actors drop out of the estimate, budget goes to scenes and editing | 2: the set, shift, light, and actors sit in the estimate |
| Repeatability across a series | 5: a prompt library holds one campaign look | 3: the campaign holds the look, a new shoot starts over |

The strengths diverge: generation takes speed and price, a shoot takes acting and scene scale. We cover both sides separately below.

## Where generation wins

Generation wins on a stream of short videos with a repeating style. The first pilot video ships in two weeks: script, scene prototype, and assembly. In the [product launch promo project](https://loginai.ru/en/cases/product-launch-video) the full cycle from script to master took seven days, the cost fell by half against the previous launch, and one assembly produced five platform versions: 16:9, 9:16, 1:1, and short cuts.

Speed rests on a shared source. The script and storyboard come from one brief, frames are built from text or an image, and voice and captions are laid on top. One master spreads into vertical, square, and horizontal, so a new platform gets its format from a ready assembly rather than a new shift.

A series pays off on repeats. A prompt library fixes light, angle, and palette, so a campaign of ten videos ships in one look. The cost per frame is set by the batch size: retouching and cropping run in a stream, and the unit price falls as the series grows. We covered the economics of post-production in the [content pipeline breakdown](https://loginai.ru/en/news/content-pipeline-economics).

The cost of a video is transparent: the budget goes to scene payment and editing, while the studio, actors, and logistics leave the estimate. [Video generation](https://loginai.ru/en/services/video-generation) covers launches, ads, training, and vertical cuts from one brief.

## Where a studio wins

A studio wins where acting carries the scene. Live facial expression, reaction to a partner, the physics of a set, and improvisation call for a person in frame, and a short format does not replace that. A long story with a recurring character runs from start to finish on a shoot, while generation assembles long scenes by hand and holds short formats.

Dramaturgy and staging set the scale: extras, sets, lighting schemes, and stunts call for a crew and a set. Such a video is planned ahead, and its look holds through the campaign while one shoot runs.

Rights on a shoot read simply: every frame is shot by an actor, a contract fixes the use, and the location gives consent. Synthetic footage is not created here, so synthetic marking does not apply to such a video.

## The verdict by task

The verdict rests on three signs: the story type, the output volume, and the marking requirement.

If you ship a stream of short videos, launches, ads, and training, and you need platform versions, take generation. The first pilot ships in two weeks, a series holds one look, and the cost of a video is visible per scene.

If you need drama, acting, and a recurring character over a long run, take a studio. The model does not reproduce live expression and improvisation yet, and a crew holds a scene at any scale.

If the task is mixed, a hybrid works: the key scene is shot on set, the rest is assembled by generation, and localizations come from one assembly. From one piece you get versions in other languages and swap the captions.

A release for an EU audience adds synthetic marking under Article 50 of the AI Act to either route. On a shoot this step drops out; on generation it goes into the publishing rule.

The scale of the comparison is the scene type. On a short format generation gives speed and price; on drama with acting a shoot holds what a pipeline assembles by hand.

## Where the routes hit a wall

Generation leaves recognizable artifacts on faces, hands, and small details, so a share of takes ends up discarded. The frame style drifts between scenes, and references with settings are fixed before the start.

A long edit cycle on a scene costs real money: a new take means time and tokens. The number of iterations is capped at the script stage, so the budget stays under control.

Video generation is metered per second: the price of a second grows with resolution and clip length, and the estimate builds from the number of scenes. The budget per video is counted up front, and batch processing lowers the cost per frame across a series.

While the brand book is in progress, generation drifts into a generic tone, and the setup takes extra time. The brand rests on references and rules, so the palette and fonts are fixed before the first series.

Long video with a recurring character needs selection and manual assembly: the model holds short formats well, and long stories are assembled on set.

A shoot has its own limit: the cost grows with the number of scenes and locations, and a new shoot restarts the setup. That is why a pipeline is cheaper on serial output, and a one-off drama calls for a shoot.

Keep the measurement on three numbers: days from brief to master, the cost of a video, and the share of takes that reached the edit. They are taken before the pilot and repeated after, so the decision rests on your own figures.

## What to do

Start with a video map. Write down which videos you need for the quarter, for which platforms, and what story type each has. The map shows at once where a pipeline works and where a set is needed.

Then the prototype. Build one test scene from the brief: it shows how the model holds the brand and the product. A prototype takes days rather than a shift, and it drives the decision on a series.

After the prototype, count the series. The measurement runs on the three numbers from the previous section, and they show the unit price of a video on your volume.
