A studio video costs like a shift: a set, light, a crew, and a rig day drive the bill, and the first master lands weeks later. Generation builds the same video from a brief in days. In our launch case the cycle from script to master took seven days. The scene type and the output volume set the choice.

## Five axes and their edges

A score applies only where both edges of the scale are named. Each axis carries a concrete top and bottom, so the comparison rests on a value or a mechanism. Five axes need ten named edges.

Series work turns on repeatability. One-off drama turns on the story limit.

1. **Speed to the first master** counts days from a signed brief: a week is five points, more than a month is one. The clock covers production and editing.
2. **Cost per minute** grows with every scene: five points when the setup spreads across a series, one when each new scene calls a shift with a set, light, a crew, and actors. The fixed part of the estimate spreads across the run.
3. **Repeatability across a series** counts videos shipped in one look without a new setup: five is an assembly from a prompt library and references, one is a setup before each video. The library holds light, angle, and palette between frames.
4. **The story limit** names the scene class: five is acting, improvisation, and staging at any scale, one is a narrow format like a short product video. Drama needs a person in frame.
5. **Legal terms** count the documents before the start: five is a contract with an actor and a location, one adds model licenses and voice consent. Consent and licenses are fixed before recording.

## The matrix: generation against a shoot

The matrix leaves the mixed task open.

| Axis | Video generation | Studio shoot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed to master | 5: seven days for a launch promo in our case | 2: a shift, editing, and post-production run for weeks |
| Cost per minute | 4: the studio and actors stay out, budget goes to scenes and editing | 2: the shift, light, crew, and actors sit in the estimate |
| Repeatability across a series | 5: a prompt library holds the campaign look | 3: the campaign holds the look, a new shoot starts over |
| The story limit | 2: a short format holds well, a long story is assembled by hand | 5: acting, improvisation, and staging at any scale |
| Legal terms | 3: model licenses and voice consent, marking in the EU | 4: actor and location rights, every frame from a shoot |

A mixed task pairs a live shot with five formats, and no single column holds it. The key scene goes to a set. Covers and cuts come from one generation assembly, and a recognizable likeness needs a signed release.

## Where a studio wins

A studio takes the scenes where a person acts. Casting and rehearsal take days. A pipeline assembles this class by hand.

A shoot leads drama and acting.

Staging sets the scale across several shifts. Extras, sets, and stunts call for a crew and a location. Rights read simply: an actor shoots each frame, and the location gives consent.

A recurring character adds set days.

## The verdict by task

A stream of short videos and platform versions is covered by the pipeline. One assembly spreads into five formats, and a prompt library holds the campaign look. A pilot ships in two weeks, and the cost stays visible per scene.

A one-off drama calls for a shoot.

## Where the routes hit a wall

Generation leaves artifacts on faces, hands, and small details, so a share of takes is discarded. Iterations are capped at the script stage. Video costs more than text: the price of a second grows with resolution and clip length.

A shoot gets dearer with every location. Each new set restarts the budget.

While the brand book is in progress, generation drifts into a generic tone. A long story with a recurring character goes to a set. A new shoot restarts the setup.

One owner holds the reference set.

## What to do

Start with one quarter and one series. The input artifact is the quarterly video map. Everything begins with three measurements.

1. **Input Artifact: the quarterly video map.** Platforms, story type, and output volume; the document shows where a pipeline is needed and where a set is. The map is agreed with the team before the first generation.
2. **Baseline Metric: three numbers before the start.** Days from brief to master, the cost of a video, and the share of takes that reach the edit; the protocol is a measurement on a series of ten videos. The numbers are taken on your own volume, and a pilot shows the exact spend.
3. **Checkpoint Deadline: 14 days after the series starts.** The same three numbers are taken again; the target bracket is a unit price cheaper than a shoot shift with a rising take share. The bracket is fixed before the start.
4. **Scale-or-Stop Condition.** Scale if the unit price of a video is cheaper than a shoot shift and the share of takes that reach the edit grows. Return the pilot to debugging if the fixed part of the estimate did not spread across ten videos. The decision is written down and revisited at review.
