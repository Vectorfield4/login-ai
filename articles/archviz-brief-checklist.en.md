A final frame gets redone because of one missing point, and the bill covers rebuilding the model, a second material build and a second render. A 3D visualisation brief is twelve points that the client and the studio fix before the first polygon. The list covers dimensioned plans, wall elevations, heights, finishes, furniture, lighting, a camera list, output formats, source files, revision rounds, deadlines and the signer. Two consolidated rounds are inside the whitebox estimate, so anything found after the final render is quoted as a separate line. For a CMO this comes down to two numbers: how fast a property reaches the market and the cost per enquiry from its card. A frame delivered two weeks late moves the campaign start.

## What is at stake

A whitebox for one layout up to 80 square metres costs RUB 25,000 to 40,000. The price covers a top view and an editable scene. Two interior views add RUB 14,000 to 24,000, a 2D plan costs RUB 5,000 to 8,000, and a model with browser viewing costs RUB 120,000 to 192,000. A missing brief point pushes the work back one stage inside that estimate.

Accuracy rests on drawing readability and confirmed dimensions. Approximate values are recorded separately and agreed before modelling: scale cannot be recovered from a picture without dimensions.

## What to prepare

- Plans in DWG or PDF, wall elevations, room heights.
- A finish specification with product codes and tile or board formats.
- A furniture selection with dimensions: catalogue items, or the sizes used to select them.
- Two or three presentation references, so lighting and camera angle are discussed on an image.
- A budget and a floor area: they decide between a whitebox at RUB 25,000 to 40,000 and a scene at RUB 120,000 to 192,000.
- A calendar with a test-frame date and one signer.

Preparation check: every item has a file and a date. An item without a file becomes an assumption, and the assumption is signed before the start.

## The twelve brief points

1. **Dimensioned plans.** DWG or PDF, units in millimetres or metres, orientation to cardinal points, one reference wall dimension. Check: the sum of the rooms matches the area in the client schedule.
2. **Wall elevations.** What sits on each wall: windows, doors, niches, radiators, ventilation shafts, service outlets. Check: every wall has an elevation or a note that it follows the plan.
3. **Heights.** Clear ceiling height, floor level, opening heights and sill heights. Check: three numbers per room, for the ceiling, the floor and the sill.
4. **Finish specification.** Material, product code, tile or board format, laying direction, joint width. Check: every surface in the frame has a material with a code.
5. **Furniture and equipment.** Catalogue items with dimensions, and custom pieces on a separate line with a manufacturing lead time. Check: every item has a dimension and a model reference.
6. **Lighting scenarios.** Daylight, evening and mixed light, with fittings placed on the plan, including output and colour temperature. Check: the scenario list matches the frame list.
7. **Camera list.** Angle, position, direction, focal length, what enters the frame and what stays outside it. Check: every frame is described in one line and approved before the test render.
8. **Output formats.** JPG or PNG, resolution, aspect ratio, and the destination: website, presentation or print. Check: every frame has a destination and a resolution.
9. **Source files.** MAX or BLEND, texture maps, application versions, who receives the source after handover, and which file counts as an export. Check: the scene opens on the client machine with all maps attached.
10. **Revision rounds.** Two consolidated rounds within the agreed scope. A new camera, a new material and a new lighting scenario are quoted separately. Check: the brief states that a round is one collected comment list.
11. **Deadlines.** A date for test frames, a date for finish and lighting approval, a date for the final render. Check: three dates in the calendar. The phrase "by launch" does not count as a date.
12. **The signer.** One person with the authority to approve camera, finishes and lighting, whose comments enter production. Check: the name is in the brief, and that person is the one collecting comments.

## Where the brief breaks

Five of the twelve points produce the most expensive rework, because each one surfaces at its own stage and sends the work back.

| Missing point | What gets redone | What pays for it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dimensions | The model is rebuilt from contours to furniture | New geometry and a fresh check against the plan |
| Heights | Furniture and openings land on the wrong levels | Rebuilt rooms and a new test frame |
| Finishes | Materials change after the test render | A new material round and a second render |
| Cameras | An approved frame moves to a different viewpoint | Recomputed frame and a new check at 100% |
| Signer | Two rounds go to conflicting comments | A third round beyond the estimate |

The stage sets the price of a change. A dimension error surfaces in geometry, a finish error at the test frame, and a camera error only in the final render, when a change costs the most.

An existing scene from a designer is a separate case. The studio checks the version, the availability of maps and plugins, the units and the object structure. It then records which file remains the source. Rebuilding materials between 3ds Max, Blender and the browser is scoped as its own line.

## An exterior adds three lines

A site plan and a facade use the same twelve points, but three of them change content. A site plan split into sectors replaces wall elevations: buildings, roads, paths, parking and planted areas. Storey levels and parapet height join the ceiling height. Landscaping joins the furniture selection: entrance groups and outdoor lighting.

An exterior check runs along the joints: a path arrives at the entrance, parking does not block a driveway, surface boundaries meet across sectors. A flat colour partition of a site plan and a volumetric scene are different production stages, so the brief picks one.

## Checking the brief

The brief is ready when a 3D modeller who has not spoken to the client reads it and asks no questions. Each of the twelve points ends in a file, a number or a name. The second test runs backwards: take the last completed property and find three points its brief did not have. If that property was reworked, the reason is already written down.

## The boundary

The boundary, a third round and a camera change after the final render, is written into the brief before the start, so the bill for rework is not discussed after the frame.

## What to do

1. **Input Artifact: a twelve-row brief table.** Rows are the points, columns are the file, the owner and the date. An empty cell becomes an assumption with a signature.
2. **Baseline Metric: two numbers from the last property.** How many revision rounds it took and how many days passed from the test frame to the final render. Protocol: the message thread and the project calendar.
3. **Checkpoint Deadline: the test-frame date.** Camera, finishes and lighting are approved by then. After that, only items on the comment list change.
4. **Scale-or-Stop Condition.** Repeat the scheme on the next property if it stayed within two rounds. Stop and rebuild the brief if a new point appears after the final render: the table was missing a row.

The scheme works on one property at a time. A set of five frames in three presentation formats for a residential developer raised apartment sales by 20%. That growth belongs to the whole set and the sales team; the case records no separate measurement for a single frame.
